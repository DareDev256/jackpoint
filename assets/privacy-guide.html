privacy tune-up

Keep This MacBook Private

A plain-language privacy tune-up for your Mac. Shrink the trackers, the ads, and the data leaks — without breaking anything you actually use. Honest about what works and what doesn't.
00 · START HERE

"Untrackable" is a myth. "A lot less tracked" is real.

Let's be honest up front: no setting, app, or trick makes a computer impossible to track. If anyone sells you that, they're lying. Your internet provider knows you have an account. Apple knows the Mac exists. Your bank, your email, the websites you log into — they all know it's you, because you told them who you are.

What you CAN do is dramatically shrink your footprint. Think of it like closing the blinds on your house. Closed blinds don't make the house disappear — but the random person walking by can't see what you're doing inside anymore. That's the realistic goal here: stop advertisers, apps, and the local coffee-shop WiFi from quietly watching over your shoulder.

Everything in this guide is legitimate personal privacy hardening. None of it hides you from anyone you owe honesty to. It just turns off the stuff that's collecting data on you by default — usually to sell ads.

This takes about 30-45 minutes. Go top to bottom. You can stop at any section and you'll still be better off than when you started. The single most impactful one is probably the firewall (section 06) — but read the tradeoff in section 08 before you touch "Find My."

01 · LOCATION

Turn off location tracking — keep the two that matter

Your Mac figures out where it is mostly from the WiFi networks around it. Plenty of apps ask for that location and there's rarely a good reason to hand it over on a laptop that sits on your desk.

Open System Settings (the gear icon) and go to Privacy & Security, then Location Services. You'll see a master switch at the top and a list of apps below it.

You have two honest choices:

  • Simplest: flip the master Location Services switch OFF. Done. Weather and Maps will ask you to type your city instead of knowing it automatically. For a desktop-style laptop, this is totally fine and the strongest option.
  • Pickier: leave it on but turn it OFF for every app you don't care about. Keep it on only for things you genuinely want, like Maps.

Either way, scroll to the very bottom of the app list to System Services and click Details. This is the hidden part most people miss. Turn OFF: Location-Based Suggestions, Location-Based Apple Ads, Significant Locations (this one quietly logs every place you regularly go — turn it off), and Suggestions & Search.

LEAVE ON: "Find My Mac" (your anti-theft — see section 08) and "System Customization" / "Setting Time Zone" so your clock stays correct. Those two are worth keeping.

Do this:
▸ System Settings ▸ Privacy & Security ▸ Location Services
▸ System Services ▸ Details ▸ turn off Significant Locations, Location-Based Apple Ads, Location-Based Suggestions
▸ Keep ON: Find My Mac, Setting Time Zone
02 · APPLE TELEMETRY

Stop the Mac from quietly reporting back to Apple

Out of the box, your Mac sends Apple a stream of "analytics" — usage data, diagnostics, and Siri voice snippets — to "improve their products." Apple is more privacy-respecting than most, but off is off. None of these affect how your Mac works. Turn them all off.

The analytics ones — System Settings ▸ Privacy & Security ▸ Analytics & Improvements:

  • Share Mac Analytics — OFF
  • Improve Siri & Dictation — OFF (this stops your voice recordings being kept)
  • Share with App Developers — OFF
  • Share iCloud Analytics — OFF

The ad-targeting one — System Settings ▸ Privacy & Security ▸ Apple Advertising:

  • Personalized Ads — OFF. This is Apple's own ad profiling for the App Store, News, and Stocks. Killing it doesn't remove ads, it just stops them being tailored to a profile of you.

The "suggestions" leak — Apple sends what you type into Spotlight (the search you get with Cmd+Space) and Safari's address bar back to itself for "Siri Suggestions." Go to System Settings ▸ Spotlight and turn off "Siri Suggestions" / "Help Apple Improve Search." In Safari ▸ Settings ▸ Search, turn off "Include Siri Suggestions." Now your half-typed searches stay on the machine.

Do this:
▸ Privacy & Security ▸ Analytics & Improvements ▸ turn ALL four off
▸ Privacy & Security ▸ Apple Advertising ▸ Personalized Ads OFF
▸ System Settings ▸ Spotlight ▸ turn off Siri Suggestions / Help Apple Improve Search
▸ Safari ▸ Settings ▸ Search ▸ uncheck Include Siri Suggestions
03 · THE VPN

A VPN: useful, but understand what it actually does

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) does one specific thing well: it hides your browsing from the network you're connected to. On airport, hotel, or coffee-shop WiFi, the VPN stops that network — and your internet provider at home — from seeing which websites you visit. It also masks your home IP address from the websites you land on. That's genuinely useful.

Be honest about what a VPN does NOT do, because the ads oversell it:

  • It does NOT make you anonymous. If you log into Gmail or Facebook, they still know it's you.
  • It does NOT stop cookies, ad trackers, or browser fingerprinting. That's section 07's job.
  • It does NOT block apps from phoning home. That's section 06.
  • It just moves your trust from your internet provider to the VPN company. So the VPN company matters a lot — a shady free VPN is worse than no VPN.

That's why the recommendation is Proton VPN. It's run by the same Swiss company behind Proton Mail, has a real no-logs policy, and — unusually — its free tier has no ads, no data caps, and no speed throttling. Most "free" VPNs make money by selling your data; Proton's free tier is the genuine exception.

To get it: go to protonvpn.com/free-vpn/macos in a browser, download the app, drag it to your Applications folder, make a free account, and click Connect. Free accounts auto-connect to a server in a handful of countries including Canada. That's all you need.

Do this:
▸ Browser ▸ protonvpn.com/free-vpn/macos
▸ Download the .dmg ▸ drag Proton VPN to Applications
▸ Open app ▸ create free account ▸ Connect
04 · ENCRYPTED DNS

Stop the network from seeing which sites you visit

Here's a leak most people never hear about. Every time you visit a website, your Mac quietly asks a "phone book" service (called DNS) for that site's address — and by default that question is sent in plain text. Your internet provider and the WiFi you're on can read every site name you look up, even on an HTTPS site. Encrypted DNS seals that envelope.

There are two easy, free providers:

  • Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 — fast, simple, just encrypts your lookups.
  • NextDNS — same encryption PLUS it can block ads and trackers across your whole Mac (not just the browser). Free for normal personal use.

The clean way to set this up is a small "configuration profile" file you download and double-click — Cloudflare offers one at one.one.one.one, and NextDNS generates one after you make a free account at nextdns.io.

ONE HONEST CAVEAT, current as of this writing: some versions of the newest macOS (26.1 / Tahoe) have a bug where these profile files fail to install with a "VPN Service" error. If that happens to you, don't fight it. Two fallbacks: (a) use the NextDNS or Cloudflare WARP app from their website instead of the profile, or (b) set DNS manually in System Settings ▸ Network ▸ (your WiFi) ▸ Details ▸ DNS and add 1.1.1.1 and 1.0.0.1 — note the manual method changes WHO answers but doesn't encrypt the question, so prefer the app if the profile won't install. Check the provider's site for the current macOS-26 status before assuming it's broken.

Do this:
▸ Cloudflare profile: visit one.one.one.one ▸ download macOS profile ▸ double-click ▸ Install
▸ NextDNS: nextdns.io ▸ free account ▸ download Apple config profile
▸ Manual fallback: System Settings ▸ Network ▸ Wi-Fi ▸ Details ▸ DNS ▸ add 1.1.1.1 and 1.0.0.1
▸ If profile won't install on macOS 26.1: use the NextDNS or Cloudflare WARP app instead
05 · THE FIREWALL

See — and block — apps that phone home

This is the section with the biggest payoff, and it's eye-opening. Your Mac has dozens of apps that quietly send data out to the internet in the background — to their makers, to ad networks, to analytics companies. A firewall like this sits at the door and asks YOU before any app is allowed to call out.

The recommendation for a non-technical user is LuLu. It's made by Objective-See (a respected Mac security researcher), it's completely free, and it's open-source — meaning experts can inspect it, so you're not trusting a black box. The paid alternative, Little Snitch, is excellent and more detailed with charts and history, but LuLu does the core job for free.

How it works in practice: download LuLu from objective-see.org/products/lulu.html, install it, and grant the permissions it asks for. From then on, when an app tries to connect out for the first time, a little window pops up: "[App] wants to connect to [address] — Allow or Block?" For normal stuff (your browser, Mail, the App Store) click Allow. When some random app or background process you've never heard of tries to reach an ad server, click Block. You'll be surprised how much chatter there is.

For the first few days it'll ask a lot of questions, then it settles down once it's learned your normal apps. One honest limitation: a handful of Apple's own built-in system services can bypass these third-party firewalls — so it's not a perfect wall, but it catches the vast majority of third-party app tracking.

Do this:
▸ Browser ▸ objective-see.org/products/lulu.html
▸ Download ▸ install ▸ grant network-extension permission when asked
▸ Then: Allow your real apps, Block unknown background phone-home connections
▸ (Paid alternative with charts/history: Little Snitch)
06 · THE BROWSER

Your browser is where most tracking actually happens

Honest truth: the single biggest source of day-to-day tracking isn't your Mac's settings — it's the web browser and the ad-tracking cookies that follow you site to site. Fix the browser and you've fixed most of it.

Two good non-technical choices:

  • Brave — blocks ads and trackers automatically, out of the box, nothing to configure. Easiest for a dad who just wants it to work. Get it at brave.com.
  • Firefox + uBlock Origin — Firefox is run by a non-profit and isn't in the ad business. Install it from mozilla.org, then add the free uBlock Origin extension (the best ad/tracker blocker there is) from its add-ons store. A little more setup, very strong result.

Either is a big upgrade over Chrome, which is made by Google — an advertising company.

The basics to set once, whichever you pick:

  • Make it your default browser (System Settings ▸ Desktop & Dock ▸ Default web browser).
  • In settings, set it to block third-party cookies and clear cookies when you quit.
  • Turn on "Send Do Not Track" / Global Privacy Control if offered.
  • Skip browser extensions you don't recognize — a sketchy extension can undo all of this.

That's it. You don't need ten privacy extensions. One good ad-blocker plus a privacy-respecting browser covers the large majority of real-world tracking.

Do this:
▸ Brave: brave.com ▸ install ▸ set as default (blocking is automatic)
▸ OR Firefox: mozilla.org ▸ install ▸ add uBlock Origin extension
▸ System Settings ▸ Desktop & Dock ▸ Default web browser
▸ In browser settings: block third-party cookies, clear on quit, enable Global Privacy Control
07 · THE BIG TRADEOFF

Privacy vs. anti-theft: you have to pick on purpose

This is the one real decision in the whole guide, and it deserves a clear head, not a default.

"Find My Mac" is a tracking feature. It lets Apple — and therefore you, through your account — know roughly where your Mac is. From a pure privacy standpoint, that's a tracking vector you might want off.

BUT "Find My Mac" is also your anti-theft system. If the laptop is lost or stolen, Find My is what lets you see its location, lock it remotely, and — most importantly — wipe it remotely so a thief can't get into your files, email, and accounts.

You cannot have both maximum privacy AND remote-wipe-on-theft. They're the same feature. So choose deliberately:

  • Keep Find My ON (recommended for most people): A stolen laptop with your logged-in email and banking is a far bigger real-world risk than Apple knowing your Mac's location. The remote-wipe is worth it. This is the right call for almost everyone, especially a primary laptop.
  • Turn Find My OFF: Only makes sense if you have a separate full-disk-encryption + backup + anti-theft plan you actually use, and the location vector genuinely matters to you. For a non-technical user, this usually trades a real protection for a small privacy gain. Not recommended unless you know exactly why.

Whichever you choose, also turn ON FileVault (System Settings ▸ Privacy & Security ▸ FileVault). It encrypts the whole disk so a thief can't pull your files off the drive even without your password. FileVault is pure upside — no tradeoff — turn it on regardless.

Find My lives at: System Settings ▸ [your name at top] ▸ Find My ▸ Find My Mac.

Do this:
▸ FileVault (turn ON regardless): System Settings ▸ Privacy & Security ▸ FileVault ▸ Turn On
▸ Find My: System Settings ▸ [your name] ▸ Find My ▸ Find My Mac
▸ Recommended: Find My ON (keep remote-wipe), FileVault ON
08 · REALITY CHECK

What this actually protects you from — and what it doesn't

You've now closed a lot of blinds. Here's the honest scorecard so you know what you bought.

WHAT THIS DOES PROTECT AGAINST:

  • Advertisers building a profile of you from your Mac and browsing (sections 02, 06, 07).
  • Apps quietly sending your data to their makers and ad networks (section 05).
  • The coffee-shop WiFi or your internet provider seeing which sites you visit (sections 03, 04).
  • A thief reading your files off a lost laptop (FileVault, section 07).
  • Casual location tracking by apps that don't need it (section 01).

WHAT THIS DOES NOT PROTECT AGAINST — and no setting can:

  • Anything you voluntarily type into a logged-in account. Google, Facebook, your bank, and your email still know it's you, because you signed in. Privacy settings don't change that.
  • Yourself. Reusing passwords, clicking phishing links, or installing a sketchy app undoes all of this. Use a password manager and be skeptical of links — that matters more than any toggle here.
  • A targeted attacker or a court order. This is everyday-privacy hardening against advertisers and snooping, not a shield against a determined adversary with resources. If you ever think you're being personally targeted, that's a different, professional conversation.
  • Your phone. Everything here is about the Mac. Your iPhone or Android is collecting just as much — worth a separate pass someday.

Bottom line: you went from wide-open to genuinely buttoned-up against the everyday stuff — ads, telemetry, app chatter, and network snooping. That's a real, meaningful win. Just don't let anyone tell you it makes you invisible. It makes you boring to the trackers, which is exactly the goal.

Do this:
▸ Bonus, biggest real-world win: install a password manager and stop reusing passwords
▸ Re-run this guide once a year — defaults reset after big macOS updates
